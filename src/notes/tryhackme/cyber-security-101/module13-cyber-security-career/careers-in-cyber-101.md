## Objective

Cyber security is **not a closed career field**. Millions of roles sit unfilled because organisations need more skilled people than exist — and the field rewards a wide range of backgrounds, not just traditional computer-science graduates. This room maps the opportunity: the **skills gap**, the **transferable** and **non-technical** skills that matter, entry-level versus senior roles, how **certifications** accelerate entry, the major **roles** (Penetration Tester, Incident Responder, SOC Analyst, Security Engineer), and how to find your own starting point.

By the end of this room you will be able to:

- Explain the **cybersecurity skills gap** and why the field needs diverse people
- Identify **transferable skills** and why **non-technical (soft) skills** matter as much as technical ones
- Distinguish **entry-level** from **senior** roles and how careers progress
- Describe how **certifications** and hands-on labs accelerate entry
- Summarise the major roles — **Penetration Tester, Incident Responder, SOC Analyst, Security Engineer**
- Understand there is **no single "best" role** — different minds fit different roles

> **The big idea:** cyber security needs people with different backgrounds, experiences and ways of thinking. There is a place for many kinds of people in the field.

## The Cybersecurity Skills Gap

Organisations continuously create security roles, but there aren't enough qualified people to fill them — because modern organisations depend heavily on the Internet, applications, cloud, databases and networks, all of which must be defended. Opportunities exist across nearly every sector: companies, hospitals, banks, governments, manufacturing, power & energy, cloud, technology, automotive, e-commerce and telecommunications.

> **Key point:** the shortage of skilled professionals means motivated newcomers who build real skills are in demand.

## Skills That Matter

Cyber security needs **more than technical skills**. Many abilities transfer in from other careers:

| Skill type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Transferable** | Problem-solving, attention to detail, communication, curiosity, persistence |
| **Technical** | Networking, operating systems, scripting, security tools |
| **Non-technical (soft)** | Communicating incidents, explaining risk, teamwork, decision-making under pressure |

> **Why soft skills matter:** security professionals must communicate incidents, explain risks to non-experts, work with other teams, and make decisions under pressure — technical knowledge alone is not enough.

---

## Entry-Level vs Senior Roles, and Certifications

Careers progress from entry-level (e.g. SOC Analyst, junior roles) to senior/specialist positions. **Certifications can accelerate entry** by validating foundational knowledge, and **hands-on labs, CTFs and projects** demonstrate practical ability — often the biggest differentiator on a CV.

`Learn fundamentals → Practice in labs → Build projects → Earn certifications → Specialise`

> **Flexibility:** your first role does not determine your whole career — people move from IT support to SOC, SOC to incident response, or development into application security.

## Major Roles

The cyber security career tree branches into offensive, defensive and research paths, with engineering underpinning them:

```text
                         CYBERSECURITY
                               │
          ┌────────────────────┼────────────────────┐
          │                    │                    │
          ↓                    ↓                    ↓
      OFFENSIVE             DEFENSIVE            RESEARCH
          │                    │                    │
          ↓                    ↓                    ↓
    Pentesting              SOC              Threat Intel
    Red Teaming             Incident         Security Research
    Security Testing        Response
          │                    │
          └────────────┬───────┘
                       ↓
                SECURITY ENGINEERING
                       │
                       ↓
              Secure Infrastructure
              Cloud Security
              Architecture
```

> **1. Penetration Tester**
> Commonly called a **pentester**, evaluates systems for security weaknesses by simulating attacks (offensive / red-team focus).

> **2. Incident Responder**
> Deals with security incidents — detecting, containing, eradicating and recovering from attacks (defensive / blue-team focus).

> **3. SOC Analyst**
> Works in a Security Operations Center, monitoring security events and investigating suspicious activity — a common entry-level defensive role.

> **4. Security Engineer**
> Designs, builds and maintains the security controls and infrastructure that protect an organisation.

> **No single "best" role:** different minds fit different roles — offensive, defensive, engineering, GRC and more. Choose based on the work you enjoy, not a ranking.

## Interview Questions

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Q1. How would you start a cybersecurity career?** | Start with fundamentals (networking, operating systems, core security concepts), build practical experience through labs and projects, document your work, consider relevant certifications, and gradually specialise based on the areas you enjoy. |
| **Q2. Why is hands-on practice important?** | Cybersecurity is a practical field; hands-on labs let you apply theory, troubleshoot problems, and understand how security tools and attacks actually work. |
| **Q3. Why is continuous learning important in cybersecurity?** | Threats and technologies constantly evolve — new technologies like cloud, AI and containers introduce new challenges, so professionals must continuously update their knowledge. |
| **Q4. Why are soft skills important?** | Security professionals need to communicate incidents, explain risks, work with other teams, and make decisions under pressure — technical knowledge alone is not enough. |
| **Q5. Does your first cybersecurity role determine your entire career?** | No. Cybersecurity careers are flexible — people move from IT support to SOC, SOC to incident response, or development into application security; skills and interests evolve. |

## Final Takeaway

Cyber security is **an open door**: a large **skills gap** means the field actively needs newcomers, and it rewards **diverse backgrounds** and **transferable skills**, not just formal computer-science training. **Non-technical skills** — communication, explaining risk, teamwork, decision-making under pressure — matter as much as technical ability. The path is consistent: **learn fundamentals, practise in labs, build projects, earn certifications, then specialise**. The major roles — **Penetration Tester** (offensive), **Incident Responder** and **SOC Analyst** (defensive), and **Security Engineer** (building controls) — suit different minds, and there is **no single "best" role**. Above all, your first job does not lock your future: cyber security careers are flexible, and skills and interests evolve over time.
