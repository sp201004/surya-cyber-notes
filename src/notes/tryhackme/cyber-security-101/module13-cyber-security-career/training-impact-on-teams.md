## Objective

Cyber security training is not just a personal activity — it directly shapes how effective a **team** and an **organisation** are. This room examines the impact of training: why it matters, how it increases team capacity and prepares people before an attack, how requirements differ by organisation size, and how to justify training as a business investment by calculating its **Return on Investment (ROI)** and writing a training proposal.

By the end of this room you will be able to:

- Explain why cyber security training matters and how it **increases team capacity**
- Describe how training **prepares teams before an attack**, helps **junior employees**, and builds **teamwork** (including via CTFs)
- Understand how training requirements differ for **small vs large** organisations and why training should be **customised**
- Build a **training business case** — treating training as an investment, not just a cost
- Calculate **ROI** using `ROI = Gain / Cost × 100` and distinguish **savings** from **ROI**
- Write a **training investment proposal** and select appropriate training (content, hands-on, relevance)

> **Core idea:** training turns a stressful, reactive team into a prepared one — the most efficient way to ramp up skills, increase capacity, and improve security outcomes.

## Why Training Matters

Training is the most efficient way to raise a team's skills, and its benefits compound across the organisation:

> **1. Increases Team Capacity**
> Trained employees can handle more, and more complex, security work — raising the team's overall throughput.

> **2. Prepares the Team Before an Attack**
> Skills built during calm periods are ready when an incident hits, rather than being learned under pressure.

> **3. Helps Junior Employees**
> Training is the most efficient way to ramp up a junior hire's skills quickly.

> **4. Reduces Senior Employee Effort**
> Centralised training means seniors spend less time individually mentoring, freeing them for higher-value work.

> **5. Builds Teamwork**
> Shared training — especially **CTFs** — strengthens relationships and collaboration across the team.

The chain: `Train → Practise → Build skills → Increase capacity → Better security`.

## Training for Different Organisation Sizes

Training requirements **depend on team size**, and generic training rarely fits well:

| Factor | Small teams | Large organisations |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | Broad, cross-functional skills | Role-specific, specialised tracks |
| **Delivery** | Flexible, individual | Centralised, structured programs |
| **Customisation** | Tailored to a few people | Tailored per team/role (avoid one-size-fits-none) |

> **Customised training** matters because "one-size-fits-none" training and **outdated material** waste money and fail to build the skills the team actually needs. **Internal training labs** and hands-on practice improve efficacy.

## Training as an Investment: ROI

To justify training, treat it as an **investment** and calculate its financial impact. The room uses:

`ROI = Gain / Cost × 100`

**Worked example (Task scenario):** a team of 20 employees, each costing $50,000/year, with training that increases productivity by 4%:

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Productivity gain (savings)** | 20 × $50,000 × 4% | $40,000 |
| **Training cost** | 20 × $500 | $10,000 |
| **ROI** | $40,000 / $10,000 × 100 | 400% |

The room's first illustrative example uses Gain = $32,000 and Cost = $5,000, giving `ROI = 32,000 / 5,000 × 100 = 640%`.

The full Task 3 calculation worked through:

```text
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│       TASK 3 CALCULATION                │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Employees               = 20            │
│ Cost / Employee / Year  = $50,000       │
│ Productivity Increase   = 4%            │
│ Training Cost / Employee = $500         │
│                                         │
│ Total Employee Cost                     │
│ = 20 × $50,000                          │
│ = $1,000,000                            │
│                                         │
│ Productivity Savings                    │
│ = $1,000,000 × 4%                       │
│ = $40,000                               │
│                                         │
│ Training Cost                           │
│ = 20 × $500                             │
│ = $10,000                               │
│                                         │
│ ROI                                     │
│ = $40,000 / $10,000 × 100               │
│ = 400%                                  │
└─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

> **Don't confuse savings and ROI:** savings (the productivity gain, e.g. $40,000) is the money benefit; ROI (e.g. 400%) expresses that gain relative to the training cost. They are different numbers.

## Writing a Proposal & Selecting Training

A training **business case** explains the problem, the recommended approach, the cost, the expected benefits, and the ROI — turning training from a perceived cost into a justified investment. When **selecting training**, evaluate the **content** (current, relevant), whether it is **hands-on**, and whether it is **relevant** to the team's real roles and threats.

## Final Takeaway

Cyber security **training** is a force multiplier for teams, not just individuals. It **increases team capacity**, **prepares people before an attack**, is the **fastest way to ramp up juniors**, reduces the mentoring load on seniors, and **builds teamwork** — especially through **CTFs**. Requirements differ by organisation size (small teams need broad, flexible training; large organisations need role-specific, centralised programs), and training must be **customised** — "one-size-fits-none" and outdated material waste money. Crucially, training should be argued as an **investment**: quantify the **savings** (e.g. a 4% productivity gain on 20 staff at $50,000 = **$40,000**) against the **training cost** (20 × $500 = **$10,000**) to get an **ROI** of **400%** using `ROI = Gain / Cost × 100` — and never confuse the raw savings with the ROI percentage. A strong training proposal states the problem, the recommended approach, the cost, the expected benefits and the ROI, and selects training on **content, hands-on practice and relevance**.
